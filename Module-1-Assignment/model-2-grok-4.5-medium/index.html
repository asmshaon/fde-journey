Why pilots stall

Remote delivery dies in the last ten meters.

The model works in a notebook. The integration fails on Tuesday at 6am when the warehouse scanner, the SSO group, and the night shift disagree. That gap is not a staffing problem. It is a proximity problem.

  • The demo never meets the floor. Operators keep a shadow spreadsheet because the new tool cannot print a label, handle an exception, or survive a shift change.
  • The vendor is two time zones away. Tickets bounce. Context evaporates. Your staff become translators for people who will never sit next to the machine.
  • Your team is already drowning. They cannot pause the quarter to productionize someone else’s prototype. So the prototype stays a prototype.

A deployment, not a retainer

Four beats. Then we leave.

Order matters. We will not staff a year-long body shop. If the work is not bounded, we decline.

  1. Week 0

    Scout

    One engineer on-site. Map the floor, the data, and the political seams. Write the kill criteria with you.

    Output: a two-page deployment brief, or a no.

  2. Weeks 1–3

    Wedge

    Ship one path that a real shift can use. No platform rewrite. Prove the coupling before we widen it.

    Output: production traffic on a thin slice.

  3. Weeks 4–8

    Harden

    Exceptions, auth, monitoring, and the second site. Pair with the people who will own it in a month.

    Output: on-call notes and owners named.

  4. Week 9

    Decouple

    We step back on purpose. If it cannot run without us, the deployment failed — we stay until it can, or we say so.

    Output: keys, not a dependency.

Who walks in

Typical embed

One lead, optional second

The lead owns architecture and the relationship with the floor. The second covers the integration surface — data, identity, or the physical interface — so the lead is not a bottleneck.
